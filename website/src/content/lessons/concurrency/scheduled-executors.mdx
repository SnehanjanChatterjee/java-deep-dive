## A delay makes a task eligible

A **scheduled executor** is an execution service that makes work available after a requested relative delay. ScheduledExecutorService can run a task once or establish a **periodic sequence**, repeated executions governed by one schedule. It retains the familiar task-result handle and service-ownership model.

The key distinction is **eligibility**: the point when a task is allowed to start, rather than a promise that a worker starts it immediately. Occupied workers, process scheduling, garbage collection (automatic memory reclamation) or a slow earlier job can postpone actual execution. A delay describes waiting policy, not a real-time appointment.

Begin with one Callable and its ScheduledFuture, the result handle for scheduled work. The first program uses zero delay so the result/ownership path is visible without a timing assertion. A positive delay adds an eligibility wait; the later sections then explain how repeated schedules calculate their next eligibility.

## Fixed rate and fixed delay answer different questions

For a repeated job, a **period** is the spacing of nominal eligibility times measured from the initial schedule. A **fixed delay** is a gap measured after the previous invocation finishes. Both methods keep successive executions of that one sequence from overlapping, but they choose different starting points for the next wait.

| Method | Next eligibility derives from | Slow task behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| scheduleAtFixedRate | Initial eligibility plus successive period multiples | Later starts can be late; the sequence does not overlap itself |
| scheduleWithFixedDelay | Previous completion plus the delay | Every completion is followed by the delay |
| Independent schedule calls | Each separately submitted request | Overlap can occur without another protocol |

For an illustrative one-second period and a task lasting 1.5 seconds, fixed rate does not run another simultaneous copy of that sequence to meet the missed second. Fixed delay instead measures its next delay from completion. These numbers explain policy, not observed performance results.

## Dry-run late periodic executions

Assume an illustrative initial eligibility at time zero, period 10 units and each invocation taking 15 units, with no other delays. Fixed-rate nominal eligibility is 0, 10, 20, …; actual nonoverlapping starts can be 0, 15, 30, … . The second invocation is late, rather than a second copy running at time ten alongside the first.

For a fixed delay of ten, completion of the first at 15 makes the next eligible at 25; completion at 40 makes the next eligible at 50. The delay is measured after completion. These are policy calculations under declared assumptions, not observed runtime timestamps or a real-time guarantee.

If an invocation throws, that periodic sequence becomes exceptional and later invocations are suppressed. A separate scheduled job can still run; the whole scheduler is not automatically shut down by one sequence's failure. A healthy periodic Future normally stays incomplete between ticks, so calling get to obtain each successful tick is the wrong result model. ## Storage, cancellation and shared state

ScheduledThreadPoolExecutor uses delayed work ordering and capacity controlled mainly by corePoolSize. Its delayed queue is unbounded; maximumPoolSize does not supply ordinary bounded admission. Many distinct delayed jobs can retain substantial state.

A cancelled delayed task can remain queued until its delay expires by default. RemoveOnCancelPolicy requests prompt removal. It changes retention, not proof of completed invocation cleanup. Shutdown policies for delayed and periodic work are configurable; account for pending handles deliberately.

Successive runs of one fixed-rate/fixed-delay sequence do not overlap and have a documented happens-before relationship, even if different workers run them. Other jobs and external readers still need a sharing protocol. A single worker serializes scheduled work, but one blocking callback can delay every other job.
